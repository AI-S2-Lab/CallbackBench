## 1. Data Collection

This section describes the data collection and preprocessing procedures used to construct Callback-1K, including corpus sources, preprocessing methods, sample filtering criteria, and data collection statistics. Our goal is to build a bilingual Chinese–English stand-up comedy transcript dataset with sufficient contextual information, providing a reliable data foundation for subsequent human annotation and evaluation of callback understanding.

### 1.1 Data Sources

The raw corpus of Callback-1K primarily comes from two types of sources:

- **StandUp4AI dataset:** A multilingual dataset for humor detection in stand-up comedy that contains transcripts of stand-up performances and provides a corpus for analyzing context-dependent humor.
- **Publicly available online stand-up comedy transcripts:** Publicly accessible Chinese and English stand-up comedy transcripts are collected to supplement the data sources and increase the linguistic coverage and content diversity of the corpus.

This study focuses on callbacks in stand-up comedy. Accordingly, data collection is restricted to transcripts of stand-up performances rather than general jokes, everyday conversations, or other forms of humorous text.

Because a callback typically draws on specific information established earlier in the discourse and creates humor by recalling, extending, or reinterpreting that information later, data collection must consider more than isolated punchlines. It is also essential to preserve sufficient preceding and subsequent context to support the analysis of cross-sentence callback relations.

### 1.2 Data Preprocessing

After collecting the raw corpus, we preprocess the candidate transcript segments through a combination of **human screening and large language model (LLM)-assisted screening** to ensure their suitability for subsequent callback annotation and evaluation.

Each candidate sample consists of a stand-up comedy transcript segment. As this study focuses on cross-sentence callback relations, screening considers not only the textual content itself but also contextual completeness, semantic coherence, and whether the relationships between earlier and later sentences can be meaningfully analyzed.

The two screening approaches serve the following purposes:

**(1) Human Screening**

Human screening primarily focuses on the overall quality of the transcripts, including:

- Determining whether a candidate segment comes from a stand-up comedy performance.
- Checking whether the context is complete and whether any content is obviously missing.
- Assessing whether the sentences are semantically connected in a reasonable manner.
- Identifying severe transcription errors or expressions that are difficult to understand.
- Determining whether the segment meets the basic requirements for cross-sentence callback analysis.

**(2) LLM-assisted Screening**

LLM-assisted screening uses the language-understanding capabilities of large language models to support the analysis of contextual structure and semantic coherence, including:

- Helping identify segments that may be truncated or missing essential information.
- Analyzing semantic connections between sentences to detect incoherent contexts.
- Helping detect severe transcription errors that may impair semantic understanding.
- Identifying candidate samples with unclear contextual relationships or those unsuitable for cross-sentence analysis.
- Providing supplementary assessments of whether candidate segments meet the data quality requirements.

By combining human judgment with LLM-based semantic analysis, we screen candidate transcripts to minimize the impact of low-quality text on subsequent human annotation.

Throughout this process, we pay particular attention to two core aspects:

**(3) Context Completeness**

Candidate samples should contain sufficiently complete contextual information to support the interpretation of potential relationships between earlier and later sentences.

For a segment that may contain a callback, the earlier discourse should provide enough background information to understand the later expression, while the later discourse should preserve the relevant semantic content. If truncation removes essential setup information or the later expression, it may become impossible to determine accurately whether a callback is present.

We therefore prioritize transcript segments with complete contextual information that can be analyzed semantically on their own.

**(4) Context Coherence**

Sentences within a candidate sample should have reasonable semantic connections and form a relatively coherent stand-up comedy segment.

Segments lacking essential context, containing severe discontinuities between sentences, or presenting relationships between earlier and later content that cannot be determined are excluded.

Importantly, **the primary purpose of data preprocessing is to identify high-quality text suitable for callback analysis, rather than to decide whether a callback is present.**

Therefore, a candidate segment may proceed to human annotation regardless of whether it contains a callback, provided that it meets the requirements for contextual completeness, semantic coherence, and overall data quality. Positive and negative callback labels, along with their corresponding relations, are determined during the subsequent Human Annotation stage.

### 1.3 Sample Filtering Criteria

To ensure data quality, we screen candidate samples based on textual completeness, transcription accuracy, content type, and contextual relationships.

Specifically, we exclude the following five categories of samples:

**(1) Truncated Transcripts**

We exclude transcript segments with incomplete contexts caused by missing content.

For example, a segment may retain only the latter part of an expression while omitting earlier information necessary to interpret it, making it impossible to reliably assess the potential setup–callback relation.

**(2) Severely Mis-transcribed Transcripts**

We exclude transcripts containing severe transcription errors that prevent the original meaning from being reliably understood.

Such errors may distort the intended meaning of sentences and interfere with the identification of semantic relationships between earlier and later content, thereby reducing the accuracy of callback annotation.

Importantly, the criterion is whether transcription errors substantially affect semantic understanding, not whether the transcript fully conforms to formal written-language conventions.

**(3) Non-stand-up Content**

We exclude text that does not come from a stand-up comedy performance.

Because this study aims to evaluate LLMs' understanding of callbacks in stand-up comedy, the data sources must remain consistent with the research setting. Introducing unrelated types of text could undermine this consistency.

**(4) Highly Fragmented Segments**

We exclude segments consisting of numerous disconnected short utterances or isolated expressions that lack coherent relationships.

If a segment lacks sufficient semantic connections to form an understandable context, it is unsuitable for cross-sentence callback analysis.

However, short sentences, pauses, and colloquial expressions naturally occurring in stand-up comedy are not, by themselves, grounds for exclusion. The key consideration is whether they compromise overall semantic coherence and contextual comprehensibility.

**(5) Multi-speaker Segments with Unclear Relations**

We exclude segments in which alternating contributions from multiple speakers make potential setup–callback relations difficult to determine.

The presence of multiple speakers is not necessarily sufficient for exclusion. The key consideration is whether their interaction prevents contextual relationships from being reliably interpreted.

If the speakers' contributions cannot be clearly distinguished, or if the semantic connections between earlier and later content cannot be established, the segment is considered unsuitable for subsequent callback annotation.

These criteria help reduce the influence of low-quality transcripts on human annotation and model evaluation while retaining stand-up comedy segments with complete contextual structures and potential analytical value.

### 1.4 Data Collection Statistics

During the initial data collection stage, we obtain **1,632** stand-up comedy transcript segments, including **815** Chinese samples and **817** English samples.

The initial data distribution is summarized below:

| Language | Number of Samples |
|---|---:|
| Chinese | 815 |
| English | 817 |
| **Total** | **1,632** |

After preprocessing through a combination of human screening and LLM-assisted screening, **1,395** candidate samples with sufficiently complete contexts, coherent semantics, and appropriate quality are retained.

The number of samples at each stage is summarized below:

| Stage | Number of Samples |
|---|---:|
| Initial Collection | 1,632 |
| After Preprocessing | 1,395 |

These **1,395 samples** serve as the input to the subsequent human annotation stage. Annotators then determine whether each sample contains a callback and annotate the corresponding setup–callback relations and natural-language explanations.

Through data collection and preprocessing, we establish a candidate corpus of bilingual Chinese–English stand-up comedy transcripts for callback understanding, providing the foundation for the subsequent Human Annotation and Quality Checking stages.
