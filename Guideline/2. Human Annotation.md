## 2. Human Annotation

This section describes the human annotation process for Callback-1K, including annotation objectives, criteria for positive and negative samples, localization of Setup–Callback pairs, requirements for natural-language explanations, illustrative annotation examples, and initial annotation statistics.

The primary objective of human annotation is to determine whether a stand-up comedy transcript contains a valid Callback relation and, where applicable, identify the correspondence between an earlier Setup and a later Callback. These annotations provide reference data for evaluating large language models on Callback Perception (CP) and Callback Reasoning (CR).

### 2.1 Annotation Objectives

Following data collection and preprocessing, we conduct human annotation on the **1,395 candidate samples** retained from the initial corpus.

Because a Callback is a context-dependent humor mechanism, annotation involves more than determining whether a transcript contains humorous content. Annotators must also examine whether a later expression recalls, extends, or reinterprets information established earlier in the discourse, thereby generating or enhancing a humorous effect.

For each sample, annotators first read the complete transcript segment to understand its context and semantic structure. They then determine whether it contains a valid Setup–Callback relation according to the Callback definition. The annotation process covers the following aspects:

**(1) Callback Presence Identification**

Determine whether the transcript segment contains a Callback.

- If a valid Setup–Callback relation exists, label the segment as a **positive sample**.
- If no valid Setup–Callback relation exists, label the segment as a **negative sample**.

**(2) Setup and Callback Localization**

For positive samples, identify:

- **Setup:** The earlier content that is recalled, extended, or reinterpreted by a later expression.
- **Callback:** The later content that performs this recall and generates or enhances a humorous effect.
- **Position:** The respective locations of the Setup and Callback within the transcript.

**(3) Natural-Language Explanation**

Provide a natural-language explanation for **every sample**.

- For **positive samples**, explain how the earlier Setup relates to the later Callback and why this relation generates or enhances humor.
- For **negative samples**, explain why the segment does not meet the criteria for a Callback.

Through this process, every sample receives a positive or negative label and an accompanying explanation; positive samples additionally receive Setup and Callback location annotations.

### 2.2 Positive and Negative Annotation Criteria

To promote consistency in annotation, we establish criteria for positive and negative samples based on the context dependence and humorous function of Callbacks.

**(1) Positive Samples**

A positive sample is a stand-up comedy segment containing a valid Setup–Callback relation. A valid Callback generally satisfies the following conditions:

- **Earlier Setup:** The preceding discourse introduces information that can subsequently be recalled, such as a character, event, viewpoint, distinctive phrase, or contextual premise.
- **Later Callback:** A later expression reuses the earlier information through repetition, semantic reference, extension, contrast, reinterpretation, or a related mechanism.
- **Context Dependency:** The humorous meaning of the later expression depends on the earlier context. Removing the relevant Setup may make the Callback harder to understand or weaken its humorous effect.
- **Humorous Function:** The reuse of earlier information generates or strengthens humor, rather than merely repeating previously mentioned content.

Importantly, **a Callback does not require identical words or sentences to recur**.

Even without obvious lexical repetition, a later expression can constitute a valid Callback if it semantically recalls earlier information and uses that connection to generate or enhance humor. Annotators should therefore consider the semantic content, contextual relationship, and humor mechanism together, rather than relying solely on keyword repetition.

**(2) Negative Samples**

A negative sample is a stand-up comedy segment without a valid Setup–Callback relation. The following cases generally should **not** be annotated as Callbacks:

- **Ordinary Repetition:** A later expression repeats words or phrases from earlier discourse without generating or strengthening humor through that repetition.
- **Topic Continuation:** The discourse continues discussing the same topic or event without establishing a humorous recall relation.
- **Simple Reference:** A later expression refers to a previously mentioned person, object, or event only to maintain narrative coherence.
- **Independent Punchline:** The segment contains a humorous expression, but the punchline does not depend on recalling earlier content.
- **Semantic Relatedness Without Callback:** Earlier and later sentences share a topic or semantic connection but do not form a humorous relation consistent with the Callback definition.

Crucially, **the presence of humor in a segment does not necessarily imply the presence of a Callback**.

The defining feature is not simply whether the later expression is humorous, but whether its humorous function arises from recalling, extending, or reinterpreting earlier information. Thus, even a segment containing a clear punchline should be labeled negative if no valid Setup–Callback relation can be identified.

### 2.3 Setup and Callback Localization

For segments labeled positive, annotators identify the corresponding Setup and Callback and record their locations in the original transcript.

**(1) Setup Localization**

A Setup is earlier content that establishes particular information or context subsequently recalled by the Callback. It may consist of:

- A distinctive word or phrase.
- A description of a character, event, or object.
- A viewpoint, identity, or situational premise.
- An earlier humorous expression or punchline.
- A complete sentence whose meaning is reinterpreted later.

**The Setup itself does not have to be humorous.** Its main function is to establish the contextual information on which the later Callback depends. Annotators should therefore identify the earlier content that is actually used by the later expression, rather than simply selecting an earlier punchline.

**(2) Callback Localization**

A Callback is a later expression that recalls the Setup and generates or strengthens humor through a connection to earlier discourse. When locating a Callback, annotators consider:

- Which later expression reuses previously established information.
- Whether the reuse operates through repetition, extension, contrast, reinterpretation, or another semantic connection.
- Whether the humorous effect depends on the corresponding Setup.
- Which location most directly represents the Callback in the transcript.

**(3) Setup–Callback Correspondence**

After locating both elements, annotators verify that they form a meaningful relation:

- The Setup must precede the Callback.
- The Callback must clearly recall or depend on information established by the Setup.
- A semantically interpretable relation must connect the two.
- That relation must contribute to generating or strengthening the humorous effect.

If the content reflects only ordinary repetition, topic continuation, or simple reference without a humorous recall relation, annotators should not assign a Setup–Callback pair.

The purpose of localization is to record where the earlier Setup and later Callback occur, providing reference annotations for subsequent model evaluation on Callback identification, localization, and relation understanding.

### 2.4 Natural-Language Explanation

In addition to labels and location annotations, we require a natural-language explanation for each sample. These explanations make the reasoning behind annotation decisions explicit and serve as reference answers for subsequent Callback Reasoning tasks.

**(1) Positive Sample Explanation**

For a positive sample, the explanation should address three aspects:

- **Setup Grounding:** What information, background, or contextual premise is established earlier in the transcript?
- **Callback Relation:** How does the later expression recall, extend, or reinterpret this information?
- **Humor Mechanism:** Why does the connection generate or strengthen a humorous effect?

Explanations should be grounded in the actual transcript and explicitly describe the relation between the Setup and Callback. Vague statements such as “the two sentences refer back to each other” or “this is funny” are insufficient without a specific textual justification.

**(2) Negative Sample Explanation**

For a negative sample, the explanation should specify why the segment does not constitute a Callback. For example:

- Words recur, but their repetition does not create a humorous recall relation.
- The later expression merely continues describing an event without humorously reusing earlier information.
- The segment contains a punchline that is understandable independently of an earlier Setup.
- The sentences are semantically related but do not form a valid Setup–Callback relation.

A negative explanation should identify the relevant missing condition rather than merely stating that no Callback exists.

**(3) Explanation Quality Requirements**

Annotators should follow these principles when writing explanations:

- **Accuracy:** Correctly describe the semantic relations present in the transcript.
- **Completeness:** For positive samples, cover the relevant Setup, recall relation, and source of humor.
- **Relevance:** Focus on the specific Setup–Callback relation under consideration.
- **Groundedness:** Avoid introducing events, character relationships, or assumptions unsupported by the transcript.
- **Clarity:** Present the reasoning in a clear and logically coherent manner.

These requirements help ensure that explanations clarify not only whether a Callback exists but also how its context-dependent humorous effect arises.

### 2.5 Annotation Examples

The following examples illustrate positive and negative annotation decisions, including corresponding locations and explanations.

> **Note:** The examples below are **constructed solely to illustrate the annotation rules**. They are **not actual samples from Callback-1K**. They should be replaced with human-reviewed examples from the finalized dataset before public release.

**Example 1: Positive Callback (English)**

**Transcript:**

> S1: My roommate treats the fridge like a museum.
>
> S2: He opens it slowly and just stares.
>
> S3: Yesterday, he visited the cheese exhibit for ten minutes.

**Annotation:**

| Annotation Field | Annotation |
|---|---|
| Label | Positive |
| Setup | S1: My roommate treats the fridge like a museum. |
| Callback | S3: Yesterday, he visited the cheese exhibit for ten minutes. |
| Setup Position | S1 |
| Callback Position | S3 |

**Explanation:**

The earlier sentence compares the roommate's use of the refrigerator to visiting a museum, establishing the unusual premise that the fridge is a museum. The later reference to a “cheese exhibit” recalls and extends this metaphor, reframing an ordinary visit to the fridge as an exhibition visit. This semantic extension creates a humorous connection to the earlier Setup. The segment therefore contains a valid Callback.

**Example 2: Positive Callback (Chinese)**

**Original Transcript:**

> S1：我爸总说，我们家客厅就像个法庭，谁回来都得汇报。
>
> S2：昨天我回家晚了两个小时。
>
> S3：我刚进门，法官就宣布开庭了。

**English Translation (for reference):**

> S1: My dad always says our living room is like a courtroom: everyone has to report in when they come home.
>
> S2: Yesterday, I came home two hours late.
>
> S3: As soon as I walked in, the judge declared the court in session.

**Annotation:**

| Annotation Field | Annotation |
|---|---|
| Label | Positive |
| Setup | S1: 我们家客厅就像个法庭 |
| Callback | S3: 法官就宣布开庭了 |
| Setup Position | S1 |
| Callback Position | S3 |

**Explanation:**

The Setup compares the family living room to a courtroom, establishing a premise in which family members must account for their actions. The Callback recalls this metaphor through the image of a judge opening a court session, reframing the father's questioning about coming home late as a courtroom proceeding. The reuse of this earlier premise produces the humorous effect. The segment therefore contains a valid Callback.

**Example 3: Negative Callback (Ordinary Repetition)**

**Transcript:**

> S1: I went to the supermarket yesterday.
>
> S2: Today, I went to the supermarket again.

**Annotation:**

| Annotation Field | Annotation |
|---|---|
| Label | Negative |
| Setup | None |
| Callback | None |

**Explanation:**

Although the word “supermarket” appears in both sentences, the second sentence merely reports the same type of activity on another day. It does not humorously recall or reinterpret earlier information. This is ordinary lexical repetition rather than a Callback.

**Example 4: Negative Callback (Topic Continuation)**

**Original Transcript:**

> S1：昨天我坐地铁去上班。
>
> S2：今天我还是坐地铁去上班。

**English Translation (for reference):**

> S1: Yesterday, I took the subway to work.
>
> S2: Today, I took the subway to work again.

**Annotation:**

| Annotation Field | Annotation |
|---|---|
| Label | Negative |
| Setup | None |
| Callback | None |

**Explanation:**

The later sentence continues the topic of commuting by subway. It does not humorously recall, extend, or reinterpret earlier information. The segment represents ordinary topic continuation and does not contain a valid Callback.

Together, these examples demonstrate that Callback annotation cannot be based solely on recurring words or related expressions. Annotators must assess the semantic relation, dependence on earlier context, and contribution to humor.

### 2.6 Initial Annotation Statistics

During the initial human annotation stage, all **1,395 candidate samples** receive positive or negative Callback labels and corresponding natural-language explanations. Positive samples additionally include the locations of the associated Setup and Callback.

The initial annotation distribution is summarized below:

| Annotation Label | Number of Samples |
|---|---:|
| Callback-positive | 710 |
| Callback-negative | 685 |
| **Total** | **1,395** |

In total, **710 samples** are initially labeled Callback-positive and **685 samples** Callback-negative.

These initial annotations form the basis for the subsequent **Quality Checking** stage, in which the correctness of Callback labels, Setup–Callback correspondence, and natural-language explanations is independently reviewed. Disagreements and ambiguous cases are addressed before the final dataset is established.
