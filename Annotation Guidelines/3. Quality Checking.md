## 3. Quality Checking

This section describes the quality-checking process for the Callback-1K dataset, including independent human review, annotation quality assessment, disagreement resolution, the exclusion of ambiguous samples, and the composition of the final dataset.

As a context-dependent humor mechanism, a callback requires understanding not only semantic connections between earlier and later discourse but also how these connections contribute to humor. Consequently, annotators may interpret the same transcript differently.

To improve the accuracy and reliability of the annotations, we conduct independent human reviews and resolve annotation disagreements after the initial annotation stage. The primary objective is to ensure that retained samples have accurate callback labels, valid setup–callback correspondences, and natural-language explanations grounded in the original context, thereby providing a reliable foundation for evaluating large language models' callback understanding.

### 3.1 Independent Review

After the initial annotation stage, **three trained annotators** independently review **all annotated samples**. Their reviews focus on whether the assigned positive or negative labels, setup–callback correspondences, and natural-language explanations are accurate and justified.

The review criteria differ slightly for positive and negative samples.

**(1) Positive Sample Review**

For callback-positive samples, annotators examine the following aspects:

- **Callback label correctness:** Whether the transcript contains a valid callback rather than ordinary repetition or topic continuation.
- **Setup localization correctness:** Whether the annotated setup identifies the earlier content actually recalled or relied upon by the later callback.
- **Callback localization correctness:** Whether the annotated callback identifies the later expression that creates or strengthens the humorous effect.
- **Setup–callback correspondence:** Whether the two annotated elements exhibit a meaningful semantic connection and contextual dependency.
- **Natural-language explanation accuracy:** Whether the explanation correctly identifies the earlier premise, the way it is recalled, and the resulting humor mechanism.

**(2) Negative Sample Review**

For callback-negative samples, annotators examine whether:

- The transcript indeed lacks a valid setup–callback relation.
- A callback involving an implicit or less obvious semantic connection may have been overlooked.
- Ordinary repetition or topic continuation has been correctly distinguished from a callback.
- The transcript contains an independent punchline that does not depend on recalling earlier context.
- The negative-sample explanation provides a reasonable justification for the absence of a callback.

Importantly, **a negative sample is not necessarily a non-humorous sample**. Even when an expression is clearly humorous, it should not be labeled callback-positive unless the humor relies on recalling, extending, or reinterpreting an earlier setup.

By reviewing positive and negative samples separately, we assess callback presence, contextual relations, and the evidence supporting each annotation, thereby reducing the impact of labeling errors on the final dataset.

### 3.2 Quality Assessment Criteria

We assess annotation quality along three core dimensions:

1. **Callback Label Correctness**
2. **Setup–Callback Correspondence**
3. **Explanation Validity**

Together, these dimensions help determine whether an annotation is accurate, well supported, and suitable for inclusion in the final dataset.

**(1) Callback Label Correctness**

This dimension examines whether the positive or negative label is consistent with the definition of a callback.

For positive samples, reviewers verify that a later expression recalls earlier contextual information and that this relation creates or strengthens a humorous effect. For negative samples, reviewers verify that no relation meeting these requirements is present.

Particular attention is given to potentially confusing cases, including:

- A later expression repeats an earlier word without creating a humorous recall.
- A later expression continues discussing the same person or event as part of an ordinary narrative.
- A transcript contains an independent punchline whose humor does not depend on an earlier setup.
- A later expression forms a valid callback through semantic echo or reinterpretation, even without obvious lexical repetition.

Therefore, label correctness cannot be established solely through keyword overlap or the presence of humor. Reviewers must consider the full context and the mechanism by which a callback is formed.

**(2) Setup–Callback Correspondence**

This dimension examines whether the annotated locations of the setup and callback are accurate and whether they form a valid contextual relationship.

A valid setup–callback correspondence should satisfy the following criteria:

- **Correct temporal order:** The setup occurs before the callback.
- **Clear semantic connection:** The callback recalls, extends, or reinterprets information introduced in the setup.
- **Meaningful contextual dependency:** The humorous meaning of the later expression is substantively linked to the earlier context.
- **Valid humorous function:** The relationship creates or strengthens humor rather than merely maintaining discourse coherence.
- **Accurate localization:** The annotated locations identify the content that actually serves the setup and callback functions.

When several candidate locations appear in a transcript, reviewers further examine the contextual relationships to determine whether the existing annotations accurately capture the callback relation being explained.

The setup and callback do not necessarily share identical words. A meaningful semantic connection can establish a valid correspondence when the later expression recalls earlier information and creates a humorous effect.

**(3) Explanation Validity**

This dimension evaluates whether the natural-language explanation accurately describes the setup–callback relation and provides a sound basis for the annotation.

For positive samples, an explanation should address the following questions:

- What information or contextual premise is established earlier?
- How does the later expression reuse that information?
- What semantic relationship connects the earlier and later expressions?
- Why does this connection create or strengthen the humorous effect?

For negative samples, the explanation should clearly state why the transcript fails to meet the criteria for a callback.

Explanations should also meet the following requirements:

- **Accuracy:** The explanation is consistent with the transcript and its annotation.
- **Relevance:** The explanation directly addresses the callback judgment being made.
- **Completeness:** The explanation includes the semantic information necessary to understand the judgment.
- **Groundedness:** The explanation does not introduce unsupported details absent from the original transcript.
- **Clarity:** The explanation is logically structured and easy to understand.

For example, an explanation such as “The later sentence refers back to the earlier sentence, so it is a callback” is insufficient because it fails to identify the recalled information and explain its humorous function. A stronger explanation specifies the earlier premise, the way it is recalled, and the mechanism that produces the humorous effect.

### 3.3 Disagreement Resolution

Because callback judgments involve contextual semantics, implicit relations, and humor comprehension, annotators may disagree about the same transcript.

When inconsistent judgments arise during independent review, the corresponding samples are re-examined and the disputed annotations are reconsidered. Disagreements may concern three main aspects.

**(1) Callback Label Disagreement**

Annotators may disagree about whether a transcript contains a callback. For example:

- One annotator may consider the later expression a valid humorous recall of earlier information.
- Another may interpret the same expression as ordinary repetition or topic continuation that does not satisfy the callback definition.

In such cases, reviewers re-examine the full context, focusing on whether the later expression genuinely depends on earlier information and whether this dependency creates or strengthens the humor.

**(2) Setup–Callback Localization Disagreement**

Even when annotators agree that a callback exists, they may identify different setup or callback locations. A transcript might contain several related earlier expressions, while the later callback draws on only some of them.

To address these disagreements, reviewers reconsider the relationship between earlier and later discourse, identify which content performs the setup and callback functions, and examine whether the original locations are accurate.

**(3) Explanation Disagreement**

Annotators may provide different interpretations of the semantic relation or humor mechanism underlying the same callback. For example, one explanation may emphasize contrast, while another emphasizes semantic reinterpretation.

Importantly, **different explanations are not necessarily incorrect**. If multiple explanations are grounded in the transcript and reasonably account for the setup–callback relation and its humorous effect, differences in wording or analytical perspective alone do not make an explanation invalid.

By re-examining these disagreements, we seek to resolve inconsistencies in labels, locations, and explanations. Samples for which a defensible annotation can be established are retained, whereas samples with unresolved ambiguity or without sufficient evidence are considered for exclusion in the subsequent stage.

### 3.4 Ambiguous Sample Exclusion

To maintain the reliability of the final dataset, we exclude samples that remain ambiguous after further review or fail to meet the quality criteria.

Potential reasons for exclusion are described below.

**(1) Ambiguous Callback Relations**

Some transcripts contain semantic connections between earlier and later expressions, but it is unclear whether these connections constitute a callback. For instance, a later expression might resemble both ordinary narrative continuation and humorous recall, while the available context is insufficient to support a definitive judgment.

If the positive or negative label cannot be justified after re-examination, the sample is excluded.

**(2) Unclear Setup–Callback Correspondence**

In some candidate positive samples, a callback may be present, but its corresponding setup or callback location cannot be reliably determined. For instance, several earlier expressions may serve as possible setups without sufficient contextual evidence to identify the relevant one.

If further review cannot resolve the uncertainty, the sample is excluded because unreliable correspondence annotations could compromise subsequent localization and relation-understanding evaluations.

**(3) Insufficient Explanation Grounding**

Some natural-language explanations rely on details absent from the transcript or fail to accurately describe the relationship between the setup and callback.

Reviewers examine whether the explanation can be supported by the original text. If the available context cannot support a reasonable explanation and the issue remains unresolved after re-examination, the sample does not meet the final quality requirements.

**(4) Unresolved Annotation Disagreements**

When further review fails to establish a defensible judgment, we do not force a positive or negative label or assign an unsupported setup–callback correspondence.

Such samples are excluded because uncertain reference annotations could make subsequent model evaluations unreliable.

Importantly, **exclusion is based on insufficiently supported annotations or failure to meet quality requirements, not on the difficulty of understanding a sample itself**. Challenging callbacks may still be retained when their labels, correspondences, and explanations can be established reliably.

This exclusion process reduces the potential impact of ambiguous or inadequately supported annotations on the final evaluation results.

### 3.5 Final Dataset Statistics

The initial human annotation stage yields **1,395 annotated samples**, including **710 callback-positive samples** and **685 callback-negative samples**.

After independent review by three trained annotators, disagreement resolution, and quality checking, **1,000 samples** are retained to form the final **Callback-1K** dataset.

The final dataset contains:

- **530 callback-positive samples**
- **470 callback-negative samples**

The dataset includes 500 Chinese and 500 English samples, distributed as follows:

| Language | Positive | Negative | Total |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Chinese | 260 | 240 | 500 |
| English | 270 | 230 | 500 |
| **Total** | **530** | **470** | **1,000** |

The overall dataset construction statistics are summarized below:

| Stage | Number of Samples |
|---|---:|
| Initial Collection | 1,632 |
| After Preprocessing | 1,395 |
| After Human Annotation | 1,395 |
| After Quality Checking | 1,000 |

The retained samples include the following annotations.

**(1) Callback-positive samples**

- A positive callback label.
- The location of the corresponding setup.
- The location of the corresponding callback.
- A natural-language explanation of how the setup and callback relate and how their connection creates or strengthens humor.

**(2) Callback-negative samples**

- A negative callback label.
- A natural-language explanation of why the transcript does not contain a valid callback.

Through the three stages of data collection, human annotation, and quality checking, Callback-1K comprises **1,000 carefully selected, annotated, and reviewed bilingual stand-up comedy transcript samples**.

In addition to positive and negative callback labels, the dataset provides setup–callback locations for positive samples and natural-language explanations for all samples. These annotations establish a common data foundation for systematically evaluating large language models in the two complementary dimensions of **Callback Perception (CP)** and **Callback Reasoning (CR)**.
